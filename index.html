How it works

  1. Ratings. Each team's goals scored and conceded per match are compared with the league average of –. An attack rating of 1.50 means scoring 50% more than average. For defense, lower is better.
  2. Recent form counts more. Past seasons are combined into a track record where last season counts fully and each earlier season counts half as much as the one after it. That track record is worth 19 matches, and every match this season counts in full on top, so the current season matters more as it goes on. On top of that, each team's 5 most recent matches count double, so the model reacts to hot and cold streaks. The same weighting applies to players' xG and minutes.
  3. Expected goals. A team's attack rating × the opponent's defense rating × the league average, with home teams given a 15% boost.
  4. Probabilities. A Poisson distribution turns each side's expected goals into the chance of scoring 0, 1, 2 or more, and combining both sides gives every scoreline.
  5. Goalscorers. The team's expected goals are split among its players by each one's xG per 90 minutes and their average minutes per match this season. A player's xG per 90 blends this season with his record in previous seasons (worth up to 5 matches, recent seasons counting more), so early-season odds don't rest on a handful of games alone. A player's chance of scoring is 1 minus the chance he scores zero.

Teams with no Premier League matches in this data start from the average first season of promoted teams, and their own results take over as they play. Players who changed clubs this season count only for their new club, measured from when they arrived. The model doesn't know about injuries, suspensions or rotation, and a player's past seasons may reflect a different role or club.
